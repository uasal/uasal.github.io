 **Chasing the Astronaut Dream**

When Yael tells people that her dream job is to become an astronaut, some puzzle and ask her whether that was just what she dreamed of as a kid or what she actually wants to do now. Yael has been pursuing space since she was a kid, and her dream is just as strong as ever. She completed her undergraduate studies in physics at UC Santa Barbara, and her graduate work has pivoted toward aerospace engineering. In all, space science has always been Yael’s gig. For Yael, studying space naturally sparked a desire to experience it firsthand: “The more I started doing the research and really understanding it, I felt like you can’t help but want to go see it for yourself.” To this end, Yael has been preparing herself in many ways, even to the point of learning to fly and working towards earning her pilot license. 

Through her whole journey, Yael’s parents have been incredibly supportive. They even took her to places like Biosphere 2 even before she was accepted to graduate school at the University of Arizona—setting the stage for her current work at UASAL.

**Where Science Meets Trajectory**

Yael now works at UASAL, and she feels fortunate to be in a lab that blends astronomy, physics, and engineering. However, her work is quite different from that of many other grad students. Only a few people in the lab are doing software similar to Yael's, as it combines both science and engineering. The engineering aspect is orbital mechanics and trajectory design, which is not something that many people do in the lab. Yael shared that “It’s cool, and also scary. Sometimes Ewan (the lab leader) says, ‘You’re my orbital mechanics expert,’ and I’ll say, ‘Expert is a strong word!’ But it’s fun, and it pushes me to learn a lot.”

Yael breaks down orbital mechanics in layman's terms as follows: “Basically, I am figuring out where to put the telescope—either around Earth or close enough to it—to best find exoplanets. Because it’s so hard to find these planets, if the Earth or Sun is in the way, you can’t see them. You can incline your orbit or go at different angles to catch the things you’d otherwise miss.”

Unlike ground instruments, space telescopes need precise positioning to maintain stability. As Yael points out, “Choosing where to put a telescope around Earth might involve a simple elliptical orbit or a transfer to what we call equilibrium points. You can go a bit farther away from Earth to a point you can orbit where you aren’t blocked by the planet, and you have a great, stable view.”

Yael also studies the orbits of exoplanets and natural bodies outside of Earth’s sphere. She utilizes software called EXOSIMS to simulate a universe. She can specify how many planets and stars there are based on parameters that define what they estimate the universe looks like. She can then begin looking for exoplanets. The process includes adjusting the parameters and looking for observations and characterizations. She observes exoplanets to see how many are in view, and then characterizes them to see if it's a Jupiter-sized planet or an Earth-sized planet in a habitable zone. 

Finding exoplanets connects to orbital mechanics because Yael compares different telescope orbits to see which one finds the most exoplanets under the same conditions. The stage after getting results is marrying orbital mechanics and exoplanet detection to answer the big questions of how cheap it is to get to orbit regarding money and propellant, but also, what is the science return? There’s no point if the space telescope isn’t able to see anything.

**A Mirror in the Stars**

For Yael, and so many others at UASAL, space exploration is a passion. Yael explains how the people she works with “love it, and they never want to stop learning. You really can’t stop learning when doing something like this. It’s really pushing the bounds of humans.”

However, space exploration is multifaceted for Yael. She is on the frontier of finding exoplanets while remaining grounded in the importance of our home planet: “Regarding exoplanets, everyone is excited to find ‘Earth 2.’ I am not ready to abandon our planet; I love our planet. As far as we know, it’s the coolest one because it has life and water.”

For Yael, her motivation for finding exoplanets is to provide another case study planet that is similar to Earth so we can understand more about our planet and ourselves. When considering potential life elsewhere, Yael expects the unexpected: “I don’t think life on other planets has any reason to be just like us. In pop culture, there’s Project Hail Mary—my favorite book. It represents extraterrestrial life as something completely different, right down to how it perceives light.” In other words, we need to find other planets to more deeply understand ourselves. The space instruments being built by UASAL are paving the way for those groundbreaking discoveries to happen in our lifetime.

In summary, the “why” of space exploration comes from Earth and from us. Yael explains how “We see storms on Jupiter or the atmosphere of Venus, and it’s exciting, but it makes Earth feel so much more special. We have life, water, and all these species. Look at a human and a hummingbird; extraterrestrial life would be so confused here. Life on Earth is so complex and beautiful in what is, as far as we know, a cold and not-yet-living universe. That makes us special. Studying space is studying us. Astronomy is about planets, but it’s really about learning more about ourselves. Biologists and chemists feel the same way—we want to know where we came from. It’s human.”
