While many of us look at the stars and see points of light, Cat Hathaway and the team at the University of Arizona’s Steward Observatory are looking for what’s hidden in between. As a PhD student in Optical Sciences, Cat plays a key role in building instruments for a space telescope designed to show us things we’ve never seen before.

---

**From Middle School Math to High-Tech Mirrors**

Cat’s journey back to Arizona is a full-circle story. Their family has long-standing roots in Arizona, and after growing up in places as far apart as Japan and Cyprus, they returned to the U of A for their undergraduate degrees. After graduation, Cat shared her learning with two years of teaching middle school math in Idaho through Teach For America. This time as a teacher taught them how to break down complex topics into simple terms which I was a witness of in our interview. After these years of teaching Cat felt the “call of the stars” bringing them back to pursue a doctorate in optics.

Today, instead of grading math papers, Cat spends their time in the Nanofabrication Clean Room. This is a specialized lab filled with precise lasers and high-tech chemicals where they manufacture the “eyes” of the telescope.

**Why “In-House” is Better**

You might think a huge telescope project like this would just order parts from a catalog, but for work on these space instruments, “standard” isn't good enough. Cat focuses on manufacturing specific optical pieces—like mirrors and masks—that are the size of a quarter! They explained that if you look closely at your hand, which seems smooth, you will actually begin to see all the texture and roughness. This is similar to the mirrors produced by companies like Nikon. They may look perfectly smooth, but actually have bumps that distort their usefulness.
- Custom Precision: By making these parts at the U of A, the team can create tools that fit the telescope’s needs exactly, rather than building the telescope around what is available to buy.
- Superior Quality: Parts made in the lab are often “levels better” than industry standards. In fact, some of the team’s manufactured pieces look “perfect” compared to blurry versions sold by big distributors.
- Special Coatings: Cat uses a machine to coat these tiny mirrors with materials like aluminum. Aluminum is highly reflective and helps prevent the mirrors from absorbing heat, which could warp their shape and ruin the image.

**Controlling Light with “Masks”**

One of Cat's most interesting tasks is creating masks. Think of a mask like a pair of high-tech sunglasses for the telescope. Because the light from a star is so bright, it often hides the much dimmer planets orbiting it.

The masks Cat builds are designed to block or redirect that overwhelming “sunlight” so we can see the “planet light”. Some of these are even “vortex masks” that spin the light like a tornado, shooting the centered star light out to the edges while letting the important images pass through the center.

**A Global Mission: Funding Cat’s Research**

Cat’s work isn’t just a big deal in Arizona; it has caught the attention of scientists across the globe. Part of their research is funded by a massive international partnership called the France-Arizona Institute (IRC for Global Grand Challenges)—a team-up between the University of Arizona and France’s national research center (CNRS). This partnership pools the world's best resources together to tackle massive questions, from climate change to the mysteries of deep space. Because Cat's work helps scientists study “Habitability Earth and Beyond”—which is all about figuring out what makes a planet able to support life—this global team helps fund their research so they can keep building the tools that look for new worlds ( https://fainstitute.arizona.edu/research/irc-initiatives ). Additionally, their work is funded by NASA and a private philanthropic group, which is another clear sign of the importance of the instruments Cat helps to create!

**The Big Picture**

For Cat, this work is about more than just physics; it's about a deep curiosity to explore the unknown. With experience in photography, Cat views telescopes as “big cameras” that allow us to push past the limits of our own eyes. Through a talk by Hongki Yoo (https://pure.kaist.ac.kr/en/persons/hongki-yoo/ ), Cat was able to articulate their motivation for work in space. Yoo described this urge for space exploration as seeking to “see the unseen.” By “seeing the unseen,” Cat and the team at the University of Arizona Space Astrophysics Lab (UASAL) are helping us understand our place in the universe, one tiny, perfect mirror at a time.
